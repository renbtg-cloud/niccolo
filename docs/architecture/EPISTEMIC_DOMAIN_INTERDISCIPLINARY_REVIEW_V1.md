# Interdisciplinary review of the epistemic domain — independent corpus and synthesis

**Scope:** Investigation of human situations, conflict, cooperation, distributed knowledge, competing explanations, context-dependent behavior, deception, and institutional processes. **This review does not compare its findings with any existing project's architecture or code.**

**Nature:** A deliberately broad, exploratory, multi-source review — **not** an exhaustive systematic review or a claim to have read every article and book in full. The architectural implications below are *interpretive syntheses* of the literature, not recommendations explicitly made by the cited authors.

**Inclusion criteria:** Contributions to meaningful semantic distinctions, interaction mechanisms, epistemic limits, empirical challenges, or substantial adversarial cases. Sources include peer-reviewed articles, meta-analyses, academic books, philosophical encyclopedias, standards, and reports by investigative institutions. Evidence was evaluated in relation to the claim type: a valid formal model does not demonstrate actual human psychology; an experimental finding does not establish an individual's traits; a professional account does not by itself establish causality.

## 1. Core findings

1. **A proposition is not an utterance:** Content, communicative act, speaker, addressee, interpretation, pragmatic function, epistemic stance, and evidence are distinct.
2. **Exposure is not knowledge:** Sending, delivery, potential access, observation, understanding, acceptance, and commitment may diverge; beliefs about other people's knowledge also matter.
3. **Individuals are context-dependent:** Learned repertoires, pressures, incentives, and cultural frames may become salient in specific circumstances; a departure from an archetype warrants inquiry, not a conclusion about intention.
4. **A group is not merely a sum of individuals:** Common and distributed knowledge, shared intentions, norms, collusion, and asymmetric power all matter.
5. **Shared informational origins matter more than copy counts:** Identical data can be independently witnessed; different documents can derive from the same chain of influence.
6. **Authenticity, integrity, and inferential strength differ:** An intact record may not be historically authentic; an authentic record may not support the claimed interpretation.
7. **Argumentation is structured and defeasible:** Attacks can target premises, conclusions, or inferential rules; burdens and standards of proof can be contextual.
8. **Inconsistency need not destroy reasoning:** Argumentation systems, assumption-based truth maintenance (ATMS), and many-valued logics provide alternatives to classical logical explosion under contradiction.
9. **Causal explanation, intention, and responsibility are not synonyms:** Benefit, causation, awareness of consequences, and intended goals are independent hypotheses.
10. **Institutional power is not the same as permission:** Delegation, practical capacity, authority, obligation, and information access have distinct semantics.
11. **Investigations change the context they investigate:** Questions, interview methods, selective disclosures, review procedures, and exposure to earlier conclusions can affect later testimony and interpretation.
12. **Missing information has a mechanism:** Non-observation, unavailability, suppression, prior filtering, and accidental omissions can radically affect the meaning of available material.
13. **Reasoning itself may be conditioned by human purpose and policy:** Support for a claim, choosing to act upon it as a premise, and permission to act are distinct questions.
14. **Useful approximation is not a flaw:** Simple heuristics can fit the purpose and costs of error, provided assumptions, limits, revisions, and counterevidence remain traceable.

## 3. Convergences and disagreements across the literature

### 3.1 Behavior analysis, theory of mind, and cognitive approaches

Functional analysis emphasizes relationships between context, behavior, and consequences. Theory of mind allows beliefs, desires, and perspectives — including mistaken ones — to be attributed to agents. Cognitive and intention-based approaches add plans and commitments. **Synthesis:** These are complementary lenses, not competing labels for one variable. Observed behavior does not prove an internal mental state.

### 3.2 Archetypes, culture, and anomalies

Cultural frames can be activated contextually (Hong et al.); situational cues can modulate trait expression (Tett & Burnett). Cross-cultural studies identify sample differences and limits to generalization; Brewer & Venaik warn against the ecological fallacy. **Synthesis:** Configurable, revisable contextual hypotheses are more suitable than fixed national profiles. An anomaly is a discrepancy relative to an explicit reference model: it may reflect genuine change, missing evidence, or a model error.

### 3.3 Bias: no single school is sufficient

Tversky & Kahneman describe systematic errors associated with heuristics; Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier explain why simple heuristics can also be adaptive, efficient, and accurate in appropriate environments. **Synthesis:** The goal is not to prohibit simplification, but to make the environment, error costs, assumptions, and revision paths explicit.

### 3.4 Childhood theory of mind and the replication problem

Wellman & Liu study progressions in understanding desires, beliefs, and access to knowledge; Perner & Wimmer study second-order beliefs. Findings about infants' implicit theory of mind have faced mixed outcomes, replication failures, and substantial heterogeneity. **Synthesis:** The conceptual capacities are useful, but a supposed universal developmental sequence — or an inference from gaze to proven knowledge — is not a sufficiently reliable basis for a computational ontology.

### 3.5 Deception: behavioral cues versus content and context

Meta-analyses of deception detection show performance only modestly above chance in typical settings, with many weak behavioral indicators; Levine emphasizes content and context. **Synthesis:** Tension, silence, hesitation, delay, or atypical behavior must not be equated with lying. They are observations that may motivate competing hypotheses.

### 3.6 Professional investigation and evidence contamination

National Academies, NIST, and Cooper & Meterko's systematic review show that contextual information presented to examiners, access to unnecessary details, and awareness of prior decisions can affect assessments. **Synthesis:** An investigative act is itself an event that may influence what will later count as evidence; access history and exposure order can matter as much as final outcomes.

### 3.7 The most plausible explanation is not automatically true

Abduction helps generate or select explanatory hypotheses, but explanatory superiority does not prove historical truth; causal and counterfactual models require assumptions. **Synthesis:** Hypothesis presentation, support, alternatives, and operational decisions can coexist without being conflated.

## 4. Adversarial cases independent of any software

**Case A — Cheng's negotiation.** A person normally negotiates quickly but activates a family-learned trust-building repertoire in high-stakes deals. In one important negotiation, he acts aggressively. Questions: changed incentives? pressure? deliberate strategy switch? incorrect cultural description? missing observations? Nationality alone is insufficient; the anomaly exists only relative to a previously explicit contextual expectation.

**Case B — Self-reinforcing testimony.** An investigator implies that the CEO has already been implicated. One witness changes their account, another investigator reads the amended account, and a third witness sees a summary. Three documents exist, but their influence paths overlap. Counting files is not counting independent confirmations.

**Case C — Authority, access, and policy.** One authority formally delegates the conduct of an investigation, another controls evidence disclosure, and a third reviews procedures. Administrative delegation does not necessarily grant unrestricted access. Investigating a policy is an ordinary domain case, provided it is authorized.

**Case D — Persistent contradictions.** Evidence supports and opposes the same allegation, from sources with different degrees of independence. An explanation must be able to state “there is support on both sides” or “information is insufficient,” while preserving justifications and alternatives.

**Case E — Intentional missingness.** An investigation sees only documents favorable to the subject because an exporter selected messages matching chosen keywords. The absence of unfavorable documents is not evidence that unfavorable events never happened.

**Case F — Strategic questioning.** “Were you alone after 10 p.m.?” might seek information, test memory, induce an answer, signal hidden knowledge, or apply pressure. The timing, previously available information, and subsequent reaction are relevant to its meaning.

**Case G — Invalid inference.** A bank transfer is authentic, the beneficiary exists, and the funds arrived. Yet the informal rule “received money from an investigated company, therefore received a bribe” is undermined by a legitimate business relationship. It is the inference, not necessarily the premise, that fails.

**Case H — Legitimate change of objective.** Management changes the purpose of an investigation without changing prior observations. The new purpose may affect subsequent task selection but cannot retroactively rewrite the nature of previous acts and decisions.

## 5. A research model, not a code model

Candidate conceptual families: agents and collectives; situations; events and actions; communicative acts and states; propositions and epistemic attitudes; sources and observations; provenance and influence; evidence and evidentiary quality; arguments and revision; repertoires, incentives, and contextual expectations; intention and collective action; norms and authority; time and information access; investigative acts; decisions and stopping conditions.

These families do **not** constitute a commitment to particular classes, tables, or services. The next investigation should examine combinations that produce real capabilities; semantic and functional dependencies are distinct from software interfaces and encapsulation.

## 7. Conclusion of this round

The selected sample covers formal theories, empirical evidence, conflicting reviews, behavioral science, institutional models, and professional investigative practice. Its result is **not** a list of mandatory new classes: it is a set of conceptual distinctions and edge cases for evaluating future ontologies.

The dominant finding is that *connections matter more than the inventory of nouns*: connections among testimonies; among beliefs and agents; among people and institutions; between behavior and context; between evidence and methods of obtaining it; and between investigative acts themselves and subsequent findings.

As the next independent research exercise, this corpus supports a matrix of multi-relational tests, looking for compositions that genuinely cannot be represented by simpler models. No conclusions about gaps in any particular software project follow from this review.
